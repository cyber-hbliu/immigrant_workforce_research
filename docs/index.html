Immigrant wages are lower in immigrant-dense areas in metropolitan Philadelphia

The question

Immigrants who speak limited English earn less than those who speak it well. Does living among other immigrants protect them, as the enclave view holds, or isolate them, as the spatial assimilation view holds? And does the difference sit in the pay a worker receives within an occupation, or in the occupations limited-English workers hold?

The data

American Community Survey public-use microdata for 2012 to 2024 in three windows. 240,089 wage and salary workers aged 25 to 64 in the 48 PUMAs of the Philadelphia-Camden-Wilmington metropolitan area, 29,299 of them foreign-born. The area models use the 7,355 foreign-born workers of 2022–2024, with New York as the comparison.

The answer

Immigrant wages are about 8 percent lower per standard deviation of area immigrant density for proficient workers and about 3 percent lower for limited-English workers. The 21 percent limited-English differential falls to 9 percent within an occupation, so most of it is occupational. Higher earners are the ones who leave the city for the suburbs.

240,000 people

About one in seven Philadelphians was born outside the United States. The largest groups come from China, India, the Dominican Republic and Vietnam, and the tail runs through Jamaica, Ukraine, Haiti, Liberia and Cameroon.

Asia and Latin America each account for roughly a third of the foreign-born. Europe and Africa make up most of the rest. Singer's 2004 typology filed Philadelphia under former gateways, cities that drew immigrants before 1930 and few afterward. The numbers since then argue for a reclassification.

The city's foreign-born population rose from 187,000 in 2013 to 240,000 in 2024, a decade high. This is a story about what those people earn, and about whether living among other immigrants changes it.

English is the steepest gradient

Among foreign-born workers, median hourly pay rises from 15 dollars for those who speak no English to 36 dollars for those who speak it very well. The step from "well" to "very well" is the largest one.

Part of that is composition. Workers who speak English very well are also more educated and have been in the country longer. Holding those things, occupation and area constant, each step up the four-point scale is still associated with 7 percent higher pay.

Between natives and immigrants the raw hourly gap is about 11 percent. Age, education and family structure explain a quarter of it. Add English and they explain 72 percent. What is left is three percentage points.

Newer arrivals started closer

Every arrival cohort starts below comparable natives and closes the gap at about 1.3 percent a year. Immigrants who came before 2000 started 21 to 22 percent behind.

Those who arrived after 2000 started 17 to 18 percent behind. The difference of 4 to 5 points is about two standard errors (p = 0.02). It runs opposite to the declining cohort quality Borjas found for national arrivals of the 1950s to 1970s. Curves stop at each cohort's longest observed duration.

Where immigrants live

The area unit is the PUMA, about 100,000 people, larger than most cities. Immigrant density peaks in Northeast Philadelphia, where the Central and Lower Northeast, Far Northeast and North Delaware PUMAs are 28, 26 and 23 percent foreign-born. Next come two suburban PUMAs in Chester and Montgomery counties at 19 percent. Every PUMA is also a dot in the scatter against foreign-born median wage, and the two answer each other. Across the 48 PUMAs the correlation is −0.37.

The same map can show where movers go. Foreign-born residents who left the city for a suburb in the past year landed mostly in the inner ring, and the circles show which PUMAs received them. The globe shows every country of birth. Chapter 11 asks who those movers are.

Immigrant geography by county

Each rose is a county and each petal one measure, all percentages on the same scale, 0 at the center and 100 at the outer ring. Philadelphia County holds the most foreign-born residents and the highest foreign-born percentage, 15.5 percent, and 31 percent of them speak limited English. Its foreign-born median wage is 21.48 dollars against 27.62 for the native-born, 78 percent, and a third of foreign-born adults hold a degree.

Salem County in New Jersey is the other end. Nine percent foreign-born, but 41 percent of them limited-English and 15 percent with a degree, and a foreign-born median wage of 17.43 dollars against 26.86 for the native-born, 65 percent, the lowest ratio of the eleven. Camden County sits between the two, with the same limited-English percentage as Philadelphia and the same wage ratio of 78 percent.

Chester County is where immigrant and native wages are both highest, 35.32 and 39.05 dollars, a ratio of 90 percent, and 60 percent of foreign-born adults hold a degree. In Bucks and Burlington counties the two medians are within a dollar of each other, ratios of 100 and 98 percent.

Denser area, lower pay

A two-level model nests 7,355 foreign-born workers in the 48 PUMAs. For immigrants who speak English well, hourly pay is about 8 percent lower per standard deviation of area immigrant density, a step of 5.4 percentage points.

For immigrants with limited English the slope is about 3 percent and not distinguishable from zero. The difference between the two slopes is the interaction the protective and isolation views argue about. It is 0.049 with a standard error of 0.029, and it points the same way in 2017–2021.

Is this just cheap housing? Native-born workers in the same PUMAs show a slope of 2 percent. Adding the area's native median wage as a control leaves the immigrant gradient at 6 percent. About a quarter of the gradient is shared with natives. The rest is specific to immigrants.

Two lines, one occupation

The vertical distance between the lines is the limited-English differential at mean density, 21 percent. Both lines fall with density, the proficient line faster.

Compare workers in the same occupation and the differential falls to 9 percent. Holding occupation constant removes about 60 percent of the differential, 40 percent of the density gradient, and all of the interaction. Most of what limited English costs is the occupation it sorts a worker into.

Which occupations limited English sorts into

The fixed effects say most of the differential is occupational. This is what that looks like. Thirty-seven percent of limited-English workers are in service occupations and 14 percent each in production and in transportation, against 12, 3 and 6 percent of the native-born. Proficient immigrants cluster where the native-born do, in management and in computing, engineering and science, and in the last of those they hold a larger proportion than the native-born.

Inside an occupation the gap is smaller but does not vanish. The right column divides the limited-English median by the proficient median in the same occupation group. In service work the two groups earn within 17 percent of each other. In sales and in construction the limited-English median is under three quarters of the proficient one. These are descriptive medians for the same sample the models use, not model estimates.

What about your own language?

Immigrant density as a whole may be the wrong measure. An exploratory check uses the density of the worker's own home language instead, for the 5,732 workers who speak a language other than English at home. Its five highest values are all Spanish, led by North Philadelphia at 41 percent of residents.

On this measure the differential does narrow, by about 5 percent per standard deviation. But look at which line moves. Limited-English wages do not vary with own-language density. Proficient wages fall with it, by about 6 percent per standard deviation. The differential narrows because the proficient earn less, not because the limited-English earn more.

The same model in the New York metropolitan area, with 54,089 workers in 141 PUMAs, gives the same picture. The interaction is 0.045 against Philadelphia's 0.046, and again the narrowing comes from the proficient side. A former gateway and a traditional one agree.

Seventeen tests, one grid

Every limited-English × density interaction the paper reports, in one plot. Twelve for Philadelphia, five for New York. Circles are generic density, diamonds co-ethnic, squares own-language, hollow marks hold occupation constant, and size is the sample. Most intervals cross zero. The two areas differ by about one standard error on generic density and by two, with opposite signs, on co-ethnic density.

With this many tests a single result at the 5 percent level is weak evidence. The Bonferroni line for seventeen tests is |t| above 2.92. Only the two New York own-language terms clear it. Philadelphia's own-language interaction, at p = 0.04, does not. The paper reports all seventeen for that reason.

Who leaves the city

In a year, 85.7 percent of the metro's foreign-born adults stay in the same house. Of the rest, 5.9 percent move within the suburbs, 2.7 percent arrive from abroad, 2.3 percent move within the city, 2.1 percent arrive from another state, 0.7 percent move from the city to a suburb and 0.6 percent the other way.

Take the stayers out and the flows are small but not random. The 2,400 a year who moved from the city to a suburb earned a median of 33 dollars an hour. The 1,900 who moved the other way earned 20. New arrivals from abroad landed in the city and the suburbs at 3,400 to 5,500 a year, earning 16 and 22 dollars. Among prior city residents, doubling hourly pay raises the odds of a city-to-suburb move by about 40 percent. In the multinomial model the estimate is 1.30 with an interval from 0.97 to 1.74.

Native-born adults move between the city and the suburbs at nearly the same rates, 0.6 and 0.7 percent. What differs is arrival. The foreign-born flow from abroad has no native counterpart, and it lands mostly in the suburbs.

Cohorts over twelve years

Following the same arrival cohorts across three survey windows, real wages rose in eleven of twelve cells. The proportion living outside the city barely moved. At the group level the data cannot detect a link between wage growth and suburbanization.

The 2010s arrivals look different, but part of that is duration. Compare them with the 2000s cohort at the same number of years after arrival and one difference holds. Latin American arrivals of the 2010s are 7 points less suburban, a difference of about two standard errors. European arrivals show the same size with too few respondents to establish it. Asian arrivals are 5 points more suburban.

Every cell, every window

The same forty-four cells as a grid. Rows are arrival cohort by region of birth, columns are the three survey windows, and color is the real median hourly wage in 2024 dollars. Hover a cell for the number. Asian-born arrivals of the 1990s and 2000s gained about 10 dollars over twelve years, the largest real gains in the table.

Switch the color to the percentage living outside the city and the grid goes flat. Most cells sit between 59 and 86 percent in every window and drift up by a few points. The exceptions are in the last row block, the 2010s arrivals, and only for Latin America does the difference from the cohort before survive a duration-matched comparison.

What this adds up to

In metropolitan Philadelphia, immigrant wages are about 8 percent lower per standard deviation of area immigrant density for workers who speak English well and about 3 percent lower, an estimate not distinguishable from zero, for those who do not. The wage difference between the two groups does not vary detectably with that density, and most of it is associated with the occupations limited-English workers hold. On the density of a worker's own home language the difference narrows, in Philadelphia and in New York, because proficient workers earn less where their language is common. Higher earners leave the city, and Latin American arrivals of the 2010s are settling inside it more often than the cohort before them did.

Analytical framework

The conceptual model and the order of estimation. Click a box for what it found, click an arrow for how the step was done. Every box links into the story and the data.

Data

American Community Survey public-use microdata sample five-year files for 2012–2016, 2017–2021 and 2020–2024, the last restricted to interviews in 2022, 2023 and 2024 so that no year appears in two windows. The geography is the 48 Public Use Microdata Areas whose interior point falls inside the 2023 definition of the Philadelphia-Camden-Wilmington metropolitan statistical area. A PUMA holds about 100,000 residents and is larger than many cities, so every area effect here is at the scale of a district.

The analysis sample is wage and salary workers aged 25 to 64 with an hourly wage between 2 and 500 dollars, 240,089 worker-years of which 29,299 are foreign-born. The 2022–2024 foreign-born sample used in the area models holds 7,355 workers.

Measures

English proficiency is the ACS self-report on a four-point scale. Limited English is the bottom two categories. Area immigrant density is the foreign-born percentage of all residents in the worker's PUMA, standardized so that one unit is one standard deviation across foreign-born workers, 5.4 percentage points in 2022–2024. Co-ethnic density is the percentage of the PUMA's foreign-born who were born in the worker's region. Own-language density is the percentage of all residents aged 5 and over who speak the worker's home language, an exploratory measure added after the main specifications were estimated.

Models

The wage equation regresses log hourly wage on the English score and controls with occupation, PUMA-by-window and interview-year fixed effects. The area model is a two-level random-intercept, random-slope specification with foreign-born workers nested in PUMAs, and its interaction between limited English and density is the main estimate. The interaction is read together with the density slope for each English group, because a positive interaction can come from limited-English workers earning more or from proficient workers earning less. Checks re-estimate on 2017–2021, add occupation fixed effects, replace the density measure, run the same model on native-born workers, and repeat everything for the New York metropolitan area.

Residential moves come from the one-year migration question, with the city of Philadelphia as one migration area. A multinomial logit with replicate-weight standard errors and a binary logit on prior city residents relate the type of move to wages. A pseudo-panel of arrival-cohort by region-of-birth cells follows groups across the three windows.

Inference

Regression standard errors are clustered on PUMA-by-window. Cross-level interactions use Satterthwaite degrees of freedom. Seventeen limited-English interaction terms are estimated across the two metropolitan areas, so any single one at the 5 percent level is weak evidence on its own. The main specifications were fixed in the analysis script before estimation.

Code and paper

One R script downloads the data through the Census API, builds every sample, estimates every model and draws every figure. Script, results and the working paper are at github.com/cyber-hbliu/immigrant_workforce_research.
